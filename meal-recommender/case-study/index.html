Overview

Question: How might I make choosing the next meal easier without requiring a complete weekly plan, a fully maintained pantry, or detailed food logging?

Meal Planner is a personal-first web app that recommends familiar recipes using available ingredients, cooking time, recent meals, and optional nutrition targets. Planning, shopping, and inventory support the decision rather than replace it with more administration.

Context Description
Project type Independent Product Management portfolio project and personal prototype
Initial user The project owner, cooking for two; lunch and dinner are the main decision points
Product contribution Problem definition, exploratory benchmarking, prioritization, interaction requirements, iteration, and acceptance criteria
Implementation approach AI-assisted implementation; technical behavior described here is grounded in the supplied implementation README
Current evidence Personal experience, one exploratory competitor trial, and reported engineering checks
Not yet established Wider demand, willingness to pay, sustained adoption, or improvement in decision time

1. Start with the actual decision

The project began with four connected difficulties:

  • Deciding what to cook when no option comes readily to mind.
  • Considering daily calories, protein, and fiber while keeping meals varied across the week.
  • Choosing something practical with ingredients already at home.
  • Translating selected recipes into a consolidated shopping list.

Cooking time emerged as another essential constraint. Breakfast was important and varied, but choosing it was usually easy. The product therefore focused discovery on lunch and dinner while allowing breakfast to inform the day's nutritional context.

The initial job to be done was:

When I need to choose lunch or dinner, help me find a meal I want to cook with the time and ingredients I have, while considering the rest of my day and recent meals.

This describes an initial user's need, not a validated market segment.

2. Benchmark an existing alternative

Early desk research considered Eat This Much, Samsung Food, and SideChef. The hands-on feedback in this project came from the owner's exploratory use of Eat This Much (ETM). A matched, completed comparative study across all three apps was not conducted.

ETM's initial presentation of suggested daily meals prompted three observations:

Observation in the owner's trial Product interpretation
Suggested meals did not reflect the ingredients available at home in that session. Kitchen context should enter the discovery flow before results are generated.
Ingredient requirements were not visible together in the way the owner needed for evaluation and shopping. Suggestions should expose feasibility, with a short path to consolidated purchasing.
The owner wanted familiar personal recipes to take precedence over the app's recipe collection. Personal recipes should be the default recommendation pool; discovery should supplement them.

The suggestions initially felt random to the owner. This is a perception of relevance and presentation, not evidence that ETM selects recipes uniformly at random. Its documented generator also considers settings and nutrition goals. Similarly, difficulty finding inventory or shopping functionality during one session does not establish that ETM lacks those capabilities.

The project consequently makes no claim to have invented meal planning or to be categorically superior to existing apps. It explores a particular workflow: personal recipes first, with ingredient feasibility visible at the point of choice.

3. Turn observations into explicit hypotheses

Hypothesis What would support it?
Familiar recipes reduce decision effort. The owner finds an acceptable meal more readily than with a mixed recipe pool.
Ingredient context improves usefulness. More recommended meals can actually be prepared without an unexpected shopping trip.
A short list reduces browsing effort. Three personal options are enough more often than not, without excessive regeneration.
Visible availability helps comparison. Users identify missing ingredients without repeatedly opening full recipes.
Optional nutrition preserves accessibility. Users can complete discovery without measurements or detailed logging; target-setting users still understand nutritional fit.

These remain hypotheses. The number of suggestions and scoring weights were design choices, not findings from an experiment.

4. Make the main product tradeoffs

Start with familiar food, retain discovery

Personal recipes were placed ahead of external recipes. The final concept allows up to three personal suggestions and up to two external ideas below them. External results must not delay personal results.

The tradeoff is setup effort: a personal-first system needs a usable library. Labeled sample recipes help people explore, but they do not prove the app will work with someone's real recipes. Measuring library-entry effort is therefore part of future validation.

Ask about ingredients without requiring a pantry audit

The proposed input became “What ingredients would you like to cook with?” rather than a demand for a complete refrigerator inventory. A shortlist, additional ingredient entry, persisted selections, and optional required ingredients make the input more flexible.

Using an ingredient in lunch does not prove it is gone by dinner. The design therefore avoids automatic stock deduction and distinguishes available, running low, unavailable, and unknown. “Use soon” is an explicit priority flag, not an inferred expiry date.

This trades stock precision for lower maintenance effort. Whether stale selections cause frequent unsuitable suggestions still needs observation.

Separate cooking for two from personal intake

Recipe quantities and shopping amounts follow household servings, defaulting to two. Nutritional assessment uses the individual's consumed or suggested portion. Adjusting a personal portion must not silently change how much food is cooked or purchased.

Make nutrition optional and uncertainty visible

Users can set targets directly or choose an estimation flow using optional personal information. Diet preferences and exclusions are independent of body measurements.

Planned food is not eaten food. The app separates actual and projected totals, labels incomplete nutrition, and does not invent missing values. The implementation has no connected ingredient nutrition database, which limits the feature until recipe values are supplied.

Keep the main journey separate from management

The original discussion favored a short preferences page. Implementation evolved into a four-step wizard: meal → servings and time → day → ingredients. This is an execution change, not a usability improvement established by testing.

The main journey is Home → preferences → suggestions → recipe details → meal selection. Recipe management, planning, shopping, and profile settings have dedicated views. Selection adds a plan entry; consumption requires a later explicit action.

The wizard may reduce visual density, but it adds steps. Comparing it with a compact form is an unresolved design question.

5. Connect the rest of the cooking loop

As the concept developed, the scope expanded beyond recommendations:

Addition Reason Deliberate boundary
Recipe-to-shopping review Reduce manual transcription and reveal purchase needs. Review quantities before adding; unknown stock is not automatically missing.
Linked shopping requirements Keep purchases consistent when plans or servings change. Preserve manual additions and avoid duplicate requirements.
Purchase-to-inventory updates Avoid entering the same purchase twice. Unknown purchased amounts do not silently satisfy known requirements.
Ingredient aliases Connect different names across recipes, receipts, and inventory. Ask before uncertain merges; preserve product distinctions.
Prepared leftovers Recognize that the best next meal may already be cooked. Reduce servings only after confirmed consumption.
Receipt import Reduce repetitive grocery entry. Review extracted lines before saving; do not guess quantities or expiry dates.

The original receipt idea became an optional provider-based import flow, with manual entry when extraction is unavailable. The implementation report describes on-device OCR as exercised, while the external vision request was not verified live.

The original idea of searching the internet also narrowed in implementation: current external ideas use a one-ingredient TheMealDB lookup. This is a useful prototype boundary, but not equivalent to broad multi-ingredient web search or verified dietary matching.

These additions made the prototype more complete while increasing complexity. Receipt scanning is a convenience layer, not necessary to test the central meal-choice hypothesis.

6. Make ranking inspectable

The implementation uses deterministic filtering and scoring rather than requiring an AI service for core recommendations.

  1. Apply meal-type, time, required-ingredient, and dietary filters.
  2. Score ingredient availability out of 50, nutritional fit out of 25, and recent variety out of 25.
  3. Add limited bonuses for use-soon ingredients and an enabled Mediterranean preference.
  4. Return the top three, using shorter cooking time to break ties.

This makes recommendation behavior explainable and testable. It does not make the weights empirically optimal. Neutral treatment of unknown nutrition avoids interpreting missing data as zero, but recipes with more complete data may still behave differently; this should be examined during use.

For zero-result states, the product explains which constraints removed candidates and offers explicit adjustments. Dietary exclusions are not silently relaxed to produce a fuller screen.

7. Evidence and validation

The supplied implementation README reports:

  • 67 browser-run logic tests across ranking, filters, unit conversion, shopping links, nutrition completeness, inventory, leftovers, receipt parsing, and persistence.
  • Manual end-to-end checks at desktop and 375 px mobile widths.
  • A real Tesseract OCR check.
  • No live verification of the external vision request without an API key.

These are reported implementation checks. Source code and test execution were not independently audited as part of preparing this case study.

No user interviews, comparative usability results, retention metrics, or nutrition outcomes are claimed. The tangible result is a documented prototype workflow and an implementation described in the project README—not proof that the product reduces decision fatigue.

8. Next validation plan

Begin with a small personal-use diary, then recruit a small exploratory group of people who regularly cook familiar meals. Keep that sample's limits explicit.

Question Observation / measure
Does the app make choosing easier? Time from starting discovery to choosing an acceptable meal, alongside a short perceived-effort rating.
Are suggestions useful? Sessions ending with a choice; first-list acceptance; rejection reasons.
Is ingredient input worth the effort? Input time, stale-stock corrections, and unexpected missing ingredients.
Is personal-first practical? Recipe-entry time and how many real recipes are needed before suggestions feel useful.
Is the flow too fragmented? Backtracking, abandoned steps, and comparison of the wizard with a compact preferences form.
Does the shopping loop help? Duplicate entries, manual corrections, and plan-change reconciliation errors.
Does optional nutrition make sense? Whether participants can distinguish actual, projected, and incomplete totals.

Use the same real meal-choice tasks when comparing against the participant's current method. Set success thresholds after an initial baseline rather than inventing performance claims now. Prioritize interpretation of observed behavior over an unsupported overall satisfaction score.

9. Reflection

The most important change was from a broad meal-planning concept to a specific decision: choosing the next meal from familiar food under today's constraints. The exploratory benchmark helped identify a personally meaningful workflow without requiring a claim of a unique feature set.

Three principles held through the iterations: personal recipes come first; incomplete information remains visibly incomplete; and automation should reduce work without making silent assumptions about stock, consumption, or purchase quantities.

The next product decision is whether these choices actually make regular use easier. Further integrations should follow that evidence rather than substitute for it.
